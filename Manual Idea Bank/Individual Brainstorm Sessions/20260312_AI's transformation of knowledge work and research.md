**2. The Collective Trap of AI Adoption in Knowledge Work**

Theory: Direct extension of Bursztyn et al. (2024) collective traps framework. Workers would collectively prefer a world where nobody uses AI (effort signals ability), but individually each worker gains from using it. The non-user externality: if everyone uses AI, your non-AI work looks suspiciously bad, forcing adoption. This is the social media trap applied to production technology.

Experiment: Lab labor market on Prolific. Workers produce outputs, employers evaluate. Randomize AI availability at the group level vs. individual level. Measure WTP to ban AI for everyone (the "deactivation" design from the social media paper, but for a production tool). If WTP to collectively ban > 0 even among users, you've found the trap.

Surprise: AI is supposed to be purely productivity-enhancing. Showing negative welfare *for the workers who use it* — because they're trapped — would be striking.

**4. The Return to Judgment: Superstar Effects When AI Commoditizes Execution**

Post-AI, the return function becomes *convex in judgment and flat in execution*. Prediction: massive reallocation, with the distribution of earnings becoming bimodal — superstars who direct AI vs. displaced middle.

Experiment: Prolific task where subjects with measured cognitive ability and "taste" (evaluated via a curation/evaluation task) use AI to produce outputs. Vary AI capability across conditions. Measure how the return to each skill changes as AI improves. Show that above a threshold, execution skill has zero marginal return while judgment skill has increasing returns.

Surprise: Most automation papers assume a single skill dimension. The Roy model prediction is that AI doesn't just raise or lower returns — it *rotates the entire return structure*, creating winners even among previously low-earners (if they have judgment) and losers among previously high-earners (if they only had execution skill).

**5. Pluralistic Ignorance about AI Use: Everyone Thinks They're the Only One Cheating**

This misperception distorts behavior: if you think you're the only one using AI, you feel guilty and hide it (investing in costly concealment); if you think everyone uses AI, you stop investing in genuine skill development. Both equilibria are inefficient relative to accurate beliefs.

Experiment: Survey knowledge workers on Prolific about (a) their own AI use for work tasks, (b) their beliefs about coworkers' AI use. Reveal true statistics (the information treatment). Measure downstream effects on: willingness to disclose AI use, investment in skill development, stated policy preferences about AI transparency.

Surprise: The wedge between actual and perceived AI use could go *either direction* and both are welfare-reducing. If people *underestimate* peer AI use, we get a concealment equilibrium with wasted hiding costs. If they *overestimate*, we get premature human capital disinvestment.

Surprise: The meta-problem — that the solution to AI quality problems (human evaluation) is itself subject to market failure — is underappreciated. The welfare cost isn't just bad AI output; it's that we *can't efficiently allocate the scarce resource (judgment) that's supposed to fix it*.
