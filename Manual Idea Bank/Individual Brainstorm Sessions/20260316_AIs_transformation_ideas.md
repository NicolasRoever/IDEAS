Cross-sectional comparisons (high vs. low AI-exposed professional markets) confound AI exposure with a hundred other things. The academic application requires journal-level data that's proprietary. A Prolific experiment can test the micro mechanism (can people distinguish AI vs. expert output? does noise in quality assessment increase?) — but that's a perception study, not a market unraveling study. You need to either find a natural experiment where AI capability jumped discontinuously in one domain but not a comparable one, or build a lab market. Both are hard.

**6. Doesn't competition among AI-augmented experts just... work?**
If everyone has AI, the competition shifts to who uses AI best, who has better judgment about when to override it, who asks better questions. The market doesn't unravel — the skill premium shifts from execution to judgment/taste. Experts don't exit; they become AI supervisors. The lemons problem requires that good experts *leave the market*. But why would they, when they can use AI too and still outperform the mediocre-plus-AI crowd? The adverse selection bite requires that the expert's marginal value above AI is unobservable. Is it?

---

**My honest read:** Objections 2 and 6 are the most dangerous. Both point to the same issue — the market has self-healing mechanisms (reputation, competition on the judgment margin) that may prevent unraveling. The paper lives or dies on whether you can formally characterize *when* those mechanisms fail. If you can show conditions under which reputation-building breaks down specifically because AI collapses the information content of output — that's the paper. If reputation just reorganizes around new signals, you have a transition story, not a market failure story.
