**Bottom line: Nobody has done "AI constructs career preferences" experimentally.** But the persuasion literature means you can't sell it as "AI influences people." That's established. You need a different conceptual object.

**2. "Isn't this just: people do whatever the AI tells them?"**

Yes. As currently framed, it is. And that's fatal. The Science persuasion paper already demonstrated this at 77K scale across hundreds of topics. Showing it again for careers is a replication, not a contribution.

So let me think about what's actually *different* about career preferences vs. political opinions...

The difference is: **political opinions have a "true self" benchmark. Career preferences don't.**

When AI shifts your view on immigration policy, we can say "you were persuaded" because you had a prior view. The shift is measurable against something. But when AI helps you "discover" that you should go into consulting vs. teaching — **there's no ground truth to shift away from.** The AI isn't overriding your preference. It's constructing one in a space where you were genuinely indeterminate.

That's the conceptual distinction: **persuasion vs. construction.** The entire persuasion literature assumes a pre-existing attitude that gets moved. Your paper is about what happens when there's nothing there to move. The AI fills a vacuum, and the vacuum is the normal state for most career decisions.

**This reframes the experiment completely.** You're not measuring persuasion (attitude shift from a baseline). You're measuring **the degree of preference indeterminacy** and showing that AI resolves it in a direction-dependent way. The contribution isn't "AI is powerful" — it's "career preferences are largely empty, and AI fills them."

**The test that distinguishes you from the persuasion literature:** Measure each student's pre-treatment preference certainty. Predict that the treatment effect is concentrated entirely among the uncertain/indeterminate. Students with strong prior career preferences should show zero treatment effect. If that's what you find, you've shown something the persuasion literature hasn't: it's not that AI overrides preferences, it's that preferences don't exist in the first place, and AI constructs them.

That's a genuinely new finding with a genuinely different policy implication. The persuasion concern is: "How do we protect people from AI influence?" Your concern is: "Most people don't have preferences to protect — so who decides?"
