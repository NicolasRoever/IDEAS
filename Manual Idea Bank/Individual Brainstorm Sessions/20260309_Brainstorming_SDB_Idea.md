This is a fascinating and genuinely hard research question. Let me lay out some thinking, going broad before narrowing.

**The core conceptual problem you're grappling with**

Your Social Desirability Atlas distinguishes three sources of SDB: material costs, social-image concerns, and self-image concerns. The existing toolkit (list experiments, randomized response, veiled elicitation à la Coffman et al.) primarily addresses social-image concerns by making individual responses unidentifiable. But as you note, self-image driven misreporting persists even under anonymity — people misreport *to themselves*. Zimmermann's paper shows exactly this mechanism: people selectively forget negative IQ feedback over time, and this operates through motivated recall. You can't solve that with privacy guarantees because the audience the respondent is performing for is *themselves*.

The AI therapy app observation is striking precisely because it suggests people share things with AI that they wouldn't even admit in anonymous surveys — which points to something beyond social-image management. The question is whether that openness can be engineered into a survey context, and whether you can prove it works.

Here are several directions the paper could take, ranging from clean-but-narrow to ambitious-but-harder:

---

**Design 1: The Zimmermann Replication + AI Interview**

This is probably the cleanest version. You replicate the Zimmermann IQ test paradigm but add an AI interview treatment arm. Participants take a cognitive ability test, receive feedback (with exogenous noise), and then after a delay you elicit recall of their performance through either (a) a standard survey question, (b) an AI interview that uses growth-mindset/self-affirmation framing before asking about the score.

The beauty is you have *individual-level ground truth*. You can measure reporting accuracy directly. And the Zimmermann results give you a sharp prediction: the selective-recall asymmetry (negative feedback forgotten more than positive) should be attenuated in the AI condition if the mechanism works by reducing self-concept threat.

You'd want a 2×2: AI vs. standard × immediate vs. delayed recall. The prediction is that the AI effect should be *larger* after a delay (when motivated forgetting has had time to operate) and should be *driven by people who received negative feedback*. Those interaction patterns would be very convincing about the mechanism.

**Design 2: The "Validation" Approach Across Domains**

Here you pick 3–4 domains where you *do* have individual-level ground truth but that differ in the relative importance of self-image vs. social-image concerns. For instance:

- *Cognitive performance recall* (strong self-image, weak social-image in anonymous settings) — you administer a test, then ask later
- *Charitable giving* (social-image concern dominates) — link to actual donation decisions in the experiment  
- *Exercise/health behavior* (self-image: "I'm the kind of person who exercises") — validated through fitness tracker data or time-use diaries
- *Financial literacy score* (self-image: "I'm financially competent") — you administer a test

The prediction: AI interviews with rapport/affirmation framing should reduce misreporting specifically in domains where self-image concerns dominate, but not (or less) where social-image concerns dominate. This would be the first evidence that an intervention can differentially target the self-image channel, and it would help resolve the puzzle in the literature about when computerized interviews increase vs. decrease socially desirable responding.

**Design 3: The "AI as Self-Affirmation" Mechanism**

This is more theory-driven. The idea: self-affirmation theory (Steele, Cohen, Sherman) shows that affirming an unrelated valued identity reduces defensive processing. You could test whether an AI interviewer that incorporates self-affirmation or growth-mindset framing before sensitive questions acts as a psychological buffer that reduces self-concept threat, thereby reducing motivated misreporting.

The experiment would look something like: participants do a cognitive test, then interact with an AI interviewer. Treatment varies the AI's conversational approach:

- *Control AI*: neutral, just asks the questions
- *Rapport + growth mindset AI*: normalizes struggle, emphasizes learning, affirms other aspects of identity before asking about performance
- *Privacy-emphasis AI*: stresses anonymity and data security (targeting social-image channel as a placebo)

If the growth-mindset AI reduces misreporting but the privacy AI doesn't (because there's no social-image concern in an already-anonymous online setting), you've shown the mechanism is self-image protection, not social-image management. That's a clean separation.

**Design 4: The Ambitious "Atlas" Approach — Estimating SDB Magnitudes**

This is the version that most directly addresses the problem you raised in your own paper — that nobody really knows the magnitude of SDB for most topics. The idea:

1. Use AI interviews with rapport-building as a *measurement tool* to get closer to truthful responses
2. Compare AI-interview responses to standard survey responses across many domains
3. Use the *gap* between them as a lower bound on SDB magnitude

The problem, as you correctly identified, is that without ground truth you can't prove the AI responses are *more accurate* rather than just *different*. But you can do several things:

- In a subset of domains where ground truth exists, validate that the AI narrows the gap between reported and actual behavior → establishes the AI as a "truth-revealing" technology
- Then apply it to domains without ground truth, using the validated instrument
- This is essentially a "revealed preference for truth-telling" argument: if the AI systematically moves responses toward ground truth where you can check, it's reasonable to interpret similar shifts in other domains as also reflecting reduced bias

This is analogous to how Coffman et al. used the list experiment as a "truthful" benchmark — except your argument would be that for self-image domains, you need a different tool.

**Design 5: The "Confession" Effect — Field Evidence from Therapy AI**

The most ambitious (and riskiest) version. Partner with an AI therapy platform. Study how self-reported behaviors and attitudes on standard intake surveys compare with what people subsequently disclose during AI therapy conversations. The therapy conversations provide a (noisy) benchmark for what people are actually willing to say when rapport has been established.

This raises obvious ethical and data challenges. But even a correlational version could be striking: show that the discrepancy between intake-survey responses and in-therapy disclosures is systematic, predictable from self-image relevance of the topic, and larger for identity-relevant domains. You wouldn't need to run an experiment — the natural variation in topic sensitivity does the work.

In the Bénabou-Tirole framework, people suppress negative information because maintaining a positive self-view has motivational utility. The therapist effectively *lowers the cost of accurate self-assessment* by decoupling "I performed poorly on X" from "I am a poor-performing person." Growth mindset does something similar: if ability is malleable, then a bad score is information about your current state, not a verdict on your identity.

Here's what exists for each domain:

**IQ — best option is ICAR, not Raven's.** The International Cognitive Ability Resource (ICAR) is fully open-source — items, scoring, and large-sample norms are all publicly available. Condon and Revelle (2014) published it with data from over 97,000 participants. You can select 8 matrix reasoning items from their item bank, and the population distributions are downloadable. Raven's itself is copyrighted and the norms are locked behind the manual, which creates legal and practical headaches for showing percentile feedback. ICAR solves this completely.

**Financial literacy — excellent.** Less than half of respondents in nationally representative US surveys get all three Big Three questions correct. The exact population distributions (percent getting 0, 1, 2, 3 correct) are published in the National Financial Capability Study, which FINRA runs every three years with open data. You can download the microdata and compute percentile bins directly. For a UK or German sample you'd want country-specific norms — Lusardi and Mitchell (2014) report cross-country comparisons. The coarseness (only 4 possible scores) is actually fine because you're not computing a precise percentile — you're computing a bin ("you scored better than X% of the population"), and with published population distributions that's straightforward.

**Empathy (Eyes test) — good for German samples.** Kynast et al. (2021) published the first age- and sex-specific percentile norms for the German RMET based on a large population-based sample of 966 healthy adults, weighted to match German population characteristics. If you're running on a German sample, this is ready to use. For a US/UK sample, Baron-Cohen's original papers report mean scores and distributions for normal adults (means around 26–31 out of 36), but formal percentile tables are thinner. You'd likely need to use the published means and SDs to construct approximate percentile bins, which is defensible given the normal distribution of scores.

**Political knowledge — this is the problem child.** There's no single standardized instrument with published population norms. The ANES asks political knowledge questions but they change across waves and are US-specific. You'd need to either use a fixed set of ANES items and compute norms from publicly available ANES microdata (which is downloadable), or construct your own battery and norm it from something like the Comparative Study of Electoral Systems or the European Social Survey.

Here's a practical solution for political knowledge: pick 5 factual items that have been asked in a recent nationally representative survey with publicly available microdata. Use that survey's response distribution as your population norm. For a US sample, the 2024 ANES has microdata. For a European sample, the ESS or country-specific election studies work. You're not developing a new instrument — you're borrowing exact items from a survey where the population distribution is already observed.

**The honest question though:** is political knowledge pulling its weight? It's the hardest domain to norm, the least standardized, and it's another knowledge/competence measure alongside IQ and financial literacy. You now have three competence domains and one moral domain (donations). If you replaced political knowledge with a second moral/character domain, you'd have a cleaner 2×2 structure: two competence domains (IQ, financial literacy) and two character domains (generosity, empathy). That's a nicer design because you can test whether the treatment effect differs across competence versus character self-image, which is a theoretically interesting heterogeneity.
