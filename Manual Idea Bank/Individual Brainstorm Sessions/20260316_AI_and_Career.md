Here are 10 pitches, roughly ordered by how much conceptual punch I think they pack:

1. **AI as Career Counselor vs. Human Intuition.** Field the same career matching test, then randomize half to receive AI-generated career advice (based on their test profile) and half to receive advice from a human counselor. Measure belief updating about career fit, job search behavior, and satisfaction 6–12 months later. The question: does AI advice move beliefs differently than human advice, and does the source label itself matter (credence goods problem)?

2. **Uncertainty Resolution and Major Switching.** Use the career test to measure the gap between students' current career trajectory and their revealed "best fit." Randomize whether students receive their results immediately or with a delay. Core idea: information that resolves career uncertainty should have real option value — do students who learn they're mismatched actually switch, and does the timing of information matter (à la Ely, Frankel & Kamenica on suspense)?

3. **Motivated Beliefs about Career Fit.** Students take the test, then you elicit beliefs about their results *before* revealing them (incentivized). The Zimmermann (2020) framework maps perfectly: do students systematically overpredict fit with high-status careers (consulting, IB) and underpredict fit with less prestigious but objectively better-matched paths? You can then randomize information provision and measure belief updating asymmetry.

4. **Willingness to Pay for Career Certainty.** Before revealing test results, offer students the option to pay (with real stakes) for *more precise* results — e.g., a detailed profile vs. a coarse one. This gives you a clean revealed-preference measure of the value of uncertainty resolution in a high-stakes life domain. Cross-cut with a treatment that makes labor market volatility salient — does macro uncertainty increase demand for personal career information?

5. **Algorithmic Aversion in Self-Relevant Domains.** Give everyone the career test, then randomize whether the interpretation comes from "our AI system" vs. "our team of career psychologists" (holding content constant). Measure trust, belief updating, and downstream decisions. The twist: career identity is deeply self-relevant, so algorithmic aversion should be strongest here. You can test whether aversion is moderated by how *flattering* the AI's recommendation is.

6. **The Generation Effect in Career Reflection.** Before taking the test, randomize students into (a) generating their own career profile predictions via structured reflection prompts, or (b) passively receiving a generic career overview. Then everyone takes the test. Does pre-test self-generation amplify the informational impact of the results? This connects your cognitive psych interest in the generation effect to a real high-stakes decision.

7. **Social Comparison and Career Aspirations.** After the test, randomize whether students see (a) only their own results, (b) their results benchmarked against the cohort average, or (c) their results benchmarked against "top performers." Does relative positioning shift aspirations, risk-taking in job search, or major choice? This is a clean test of reference-dependent preferences in a career context.

8. **Overconfidence and Sorting into Competitive Careers.** Use the career test to construct an objective "competitiveness fit" score. Before revealing results, measure beliefs about own competitiveness (Niederle-Vesterlund style). The question: does overconfidence predict sorting into tournament-style careers (finance, consulting) conditional on actual fit? The career test gives you the rare ability to separate confidence from ability in a field setting.

9. **Information Avoidance in Career Decisions.** Give students the *option* to see their career test results (rather than forcing it). Who opts out? Measure correlates: students who suspect a mismatch between their current path and likely results should avoid information more. This is a clean Golman, Hagmann & Loewenstein (2017) test in a domain where avoidance has real consequences.

10. **AI-Augmented Self-Knowledge and Welfare.** Randomize students into an AI Socratic dialogue about their career preferences *before* taking the test (your preference elicitation idea applied here). Does AI-assisted reflection change how students interpret their results? Does it improve the test's predictive validity for later satisfaction? The welfare question: are post-reflection preferences "better" by students' own later judgment?

My gut ranking for "publishable in a top-5 with the right execution": ideas 3, 4, and 5 have the tightest single punchlines. Idea 3 is the most natural fit for your existing toolkit (Zimmermann framework, belief elicitation, social desirability). Idea 10 is the most "you" conceptually but hardest to pin down empirically.

But if everyone learns their "true fit," does this improve or worsen labor market sorting? When information is symmetric, matching should improve; when it's one-sided, strategic behavior kicks in. The question is about the welfare effects of democratized self-knowledge — a genuinely new problem AI creates.

2. **Uncertainty as a Resource in Career Markets.** Flip the standard framing: uncertainty about own fit might be *valuable* because it keeps option value alive and prevents premature lock-in. Reveal career test results to everyone, but randomize the *precision* of the signal (coarse vs. fine). Measure not just major switching but application breadth, risk-taking, and exploration behavior over time. If high-precision information narrows search too early, there's a real allocative cost to cheap AI-generated self-knowledge. This is an economics paper about optimal information precision in matching markets.

3. **Coordination Failure from Correlated Signals.** Career matching AI tools give *correlated advice* — if the algorithm says "you're a great fit for consulting," it says it to everyone with a similar profile. Randomize whether students see generic test results vs. results explicitly framed as "shared by X% of your peers with similar profiles." Does learning about signal correlation change behavior? This is a congestion/coordination problem: AI-generated career advice could create misallocation by herding similar types into the same market.

4. **Preference Discovery vs. Preference Confirmation.** Students take the test, but before results, you elicit their reservation wage and job attribute rankings for their *current* preferred career. Then reveal results. Randomize whether students get a structured AI dialogue helping them interpret what the mismatch means for their economic prospects (salary distributions, unemployment rates for "better fit" careers) vs. raw results only. The outcome isn't belief updating — it's whether revealed preferences over *job attributes* (stability, earnings, autonomy) change. Do people discover they had different preferences than they thought, or do they confirm existing ones? The welfare economics question: are pre-test preferences or post-information preferences better predictors of later satisfaction?

5. **The Market Price of Identity.** Career mismatches have an economic cost (lower productivity, higher turnover), but people pay a premium to stay in identity-consistent careers. Use the test to identify students whose "fit" career diverges from their stated identity. Elicit willingness-to-accept for switching: how large a salary premium would it take to move you toward your better-fit career? This gives you a revealed-preference measure of the *cost of identity* in career markets — a quantity that matters for allocation efficiency but nobody has cleanly measured.

6. **Strategic Information Acquisition in Thin Markets.** Not all career information is equally valuable — learning your fit for a competitive field is more strategically important than for an uncompetitive one. Before revealing results, let students *choose which dimensions of fit* to learn about (e.g., analytical, interpersonal, creative). The portfolio of information they acquire reveals beliefs about which margins matter. The economics: in a world of cheap AI-generated information, *what* people choose to learn — not whether they learn — becomes the scarce decision.

7. **Equilibrium Effects of Belief Harmonization.** If an AI tool gives everyone accurate self-knowledge simultaneously, the composition of applicant pools changes. Run the career test on your full sample, but randomize at the *session level* whether results are revealed to everyone vs. a random subset. Compare job search behavior and application patterns across treatment groups. The macro question: does simultaneous information revelation improve or worsen matching efficiency when candidates respond to the same signal?

8. **The Discount Rate of Career Information.** Reveal career test results to everyone, but randomize the *time horizon framing*: "this is your fit for your first job" vs. "this is your fit for your career at age 40." If people discount long-run fit heavily, they'll ignore mismatch information when it's framed as distant. This connects to hyperbolic discounting in human capital investment — and AI tools typically give you a static fit score without specifying the horizon, which matters for how people use it.

9. **Comparative Advantage Awareness and Specialization.** Students learn their absolute fit scores, but randomize whether they also learn their *comparative advantage* — where their relative edge is largest across career dimensions. Standard career tools give absolute fit; economic logic says comparative advantage should drive sorting. Does reframing results in comparative-advantage terms change career choices? If so, current AI career tools are systematically presenting information in the wrong economic framework, causing misallocation.

10. **Selling Your Signal.** After learning their career test results, offer students the option to *disclose* their fit score to potential employers/internship programs (make it consequential). Who discloses? Standard unraveling logic (Milgrom, Grossman) says everyone should — but if career identity or motivated beliefs prevent it, you get partial unraveling. AI makes self-knowledge cheap, but the *disclosure decision* is the economically interesting margin. The question: does cheap self-knowledge actually improve market efficiency, or do behavioral frictions prevent the information from reaching the demand side?

Here are 8 sharper pitches:

1. **The Congestion Externality of AI Career Advice.** AI career tools give *correlated* recommendations — similar people get similar advice. This is a textbook negative externality: my AI-informed career choice imposes a congestion cost on everyone with a similar profile. Design: everyone takes the career test. Randomize whether students also learn the *supply-side* — the distribution of career slots and how many peers are chasing the same "best fit" path. You're not studying social comparison (individual psych) — you're studying whether students internalize the externality when given market-level information. The punchline: AI self-knowledge is individually optimal but can worsen allocative efficiency without supply-side information. This is a missing-market problem that AI creates.

2. **An Information Arms Race in Career Markets.** Create a *competitive* allocation game: students compete for a limited number of desirable "internship slots" in a lab-in-the-field setting. Before competing, they can acquire career-fit information (from the test) at some cost. The strategic question: if your competitors are informed, you *must* be too — even if the information per se doesn't help you compete, it helps you target. Randomize whether the acquisition decision is simultaneous vs. sequential, and whether competitors' information status is observable. This gives you a clean test of strategic complementarity in information acquisition — the mechanism through which AI career tools could trigger wasteful arms races even when the information itself is accurate.

3. **Comparative Advantage Framing in a Matching Market.** Run an actual incentivized matching game: students rank simulated "careers" (with real payoff consequences), and careers have capacity constraints. Everyone gets the same career test data, but randomize the *framing*: absolute fit scores ("you're a 7/10 for consulting") vs. comparative advantage scores ("consulting is where your relative edge over other careers is largest"). Standard theory: comparative advantage should drive efficient sorting, absolute advantage shouldn't. If framing changes sorting in the matching market, then the way AI career tools present information has *allocative* consequences — current tools are systematically misframing the decision and causing inefficiency.

4. **Preference Discovery and Demand for Career Attributes.** AI Socratic dialogue helps students surface preferences they didn't know they had. The paper isn't about "better decisions" — it's about whether AI-assisted preference discovery *shifts demand curves* for career attributes, and whether the pre- or post-reflection demand curve better predicts revealed preference six months later (the Bernheim-Rangel adjudication you've been thinking about).
